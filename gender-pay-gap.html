Why Women in STEM Are Still Paid Less and Why It Is Getting Worse

STEM fields - science, technology, engineering, and mathematics - are often seen as some of the most objective and fair areas of today's economy. The idea is simple: if you work hard and deliver results, you will be rewarded fairly, no matter who you are. Yet the data tells a different story. Women in STEM earn on average 16% less than their male colleagues, and what makes this figure especially hard to ignore is that the same gap was only 10.5% back in 2010 [3]. In a sector that considers itself a symbol of progress, the gender pay gap has not shrunk - it has grown.

Understanding why this is happening requires looking at the problem from several angles, as no single cause can fully explain it. The gap is the result of overlapping forces - cultural, structural, and economic - that start long before a woman enters the workforce and continue shaping her career at every stage.

The Broader Picture: Europe and Austria

Before focusing on STEM, it is worth understanding the broader scale of the problem. Across the European Union, women earn on average 12% less per hour than men [1]. Austria sits well above that average, with a gap of 17.6% - one of the highest in the EU, and a figure that has remained high for years [2].

The gap does not affect all women equally. In Austria, women with migration backgrounds face a significantly larger gap than women without, experiencing disadvantages based on both gender and ethnic or national origin [7]. This is an important reminder that "women in STEM" is not a single, uniform group - and that policies or conversations focused only on gender, without accounting for other forms of discrimination, will always miss part of the picture.


Why STEM Is a Particularly Important Case

Given that STEM jobs are among the highest-paid in the economy, the pay gap in this sector has particularly serious consequences - and makes the failure to close it even more significant. A growing body of research shows that women in STEM are not simply concentrated in lower-paying areas of the field by coincidence - they are pushed there by a combination of early socialisation, workplace bias, and structural barriers that most institutions have yet to seriously address.

Women represent around 15.9% of engineering workers and approximately 25.6% of those in computer-related roles [5]. These are not small gaps in representation, and they matter because the subfields where women are least present tend to be the ones with the highest salaries and the most opportunities for career advancement. What looks like a pay gap is, in part, a reflection of who gets to access the most valuable parts of the field - and who does not.

What the Data Shows

Qualifications Are Not Enough

One of the most common assumptions about the pay gap is that it simply reflects differences in education or career choices, and that more qualified women would naturally close it over time. But this assumption does not hold up. According to a 2024 OECD study, women with university degrees working full-time earn only 77% of what equally qualified men earn - meaning the gap among highly educated workers is actually larger, at around 23% [2]. Higher education does not protect women from the gap; in many cases, it widens it. The consequences also accumulate over a lifetime: because women earn less during their working years, they retire with significantly less. In 2020, women over 65 in the EU received pensions that were on average 28.3% lower than men's, putting them at a much higher risk of poverty in old age [1].



The Leaky Pipeline

One of the most useful frameworks for understanding women's underrepresentation in senior STEM roles is the concept of the "leaky pipeline". Women make up around 35% of STEM graduates, which is a significant share - but their presence drops at every stage after graduation [6]. Fewer women reach senior research positions, fewer move into management, and even fewer make it to leadership. What makes this especially concerning is that the pay gap between men and women in STEM can appear within the first year after graduation, before any differences in experience or career breaks could explain it [6]. This means the problem is not simply that women fall behind over time - it is that they start behind, and the gap only grows from there.


The Motherhood Penalty

One of the clearest examples of how the gender pay gap works in practice is what happens when people have children. For men, fatherhood tends to come with a salary increase - fathers with children under 18 earned 23% more than their childless male counterparts in 2023 [13]. For women, the opposite is true. Mothers face a wage penalty that averages 5% to 7% per child - and this is not explained by reduced productivity [16]. The same event - becoming a parent - is financially rewarded for men and financially punished for women. Fatherhood is seen by employers as a sign of commitment, stability, and dependability [12]. Motherhood is treated as the opposite.

In STEM specifically, the penalty arrives even before parenthood itself. Research based on interviews with childless PhD students and postdoctoral researchers in natural science and engineering found that women were twice as likely as men to abandon plans for an academic science career - not because they had children, but because workplace culture told them that motherhood and a serious STEM career were incompatible [14]. Some were explicitly told by their advisers to choose between a career and a family. Others felt pressure to hide miscarriages or deny any intention of having children, for fear of no longer being taken seriously [14]. Researchers described this as the "specter of motherhood" - the way unfounded assumptions about future caregiving can derail a woman's STEM career long before she has made any such decision [14].

The result is that women in STEM are not only penalised for having children - they are penalised for the possibility of having them.

Unpaid Work and the Part-Time Trap

A significant part of the pay gap is also driven by the unequal distribution of unpaid care work. In Austria, 51.1% of employed women work part-time, compared to just 13.7% of men. Women in Austria also spend nearly two more hours per day than men on unpaid care and household tasks [2]. This is not primarily a matter of personal choice - it reflects deeply rooted social expectations that women will take on the majority of domestic work and childcare, and that their careers should adjust around this. Part-time work means lower earnings, slower career progression, and less access to the promotions and salary increases that build up over time. The work is real - it is simply not paid.


The Negotiation Trap

It is often said that women "just need to negotiate more". However, research shows that the situation is more complicated than that. Women who negotiate salaries are frequently judged more harshly than men who do the same - while a man may be seen as confident, a woman is more likely to be viewed as aggressive or impolite.

Research also shows that women often plan to negotiate less forcefully because they already anticipate this kind of bias. Many fear that being too direct in salary negotiations could damage their reputation or even result in a lower final offer. These factors slow down salary growth for women and help keep the gender pay gap in place [9].

Where the Gap Comes From: Root Causes

Stereotypes That Start in Childhood

Research consistently shows that gender stereotypes begin shaping children's relationship to STEM long before they make any formal career decisions [4]. Girls are steered away from technical subjects not through any single dramatic moment, but through a series of small signals: the kinds of toys and activities considered appropriate for them, the assumptions made by teachers and parents, and the broader cultural message that engineering or computer science are naturally male domains. What is striking is that this happens even when girls perform just as well as boys in those subjects. By the time women apply for STEM positions, the idea of who belongs in those roles has already shaped their path significantly.

The Confidence Gap

Stereotypes do not only shape how others see women in STEM - they also shape how women see themselves. Research published in PNAS found that women with the same engineering or computer science degrees as their male peers tend to have lower confidence in their technical abilities, and that this leads them to pursue less competitive, lower-paying jobs - or to leave STEM altogether. This is not a personality flaw. It is the predictable result of years of being subtly told that these fields are not really yours. The confidence gap in STEM is, in large part, a manufactured one - and it comes with a measurable salary cost [10].

The Lack of Role Models

One of the less visible reasons why women leave STEM is the absence of people who look like them at the top. With fewer women in senior roles, younger women have fewer relatable role models to keep their interest and ambition alive - which contributes to both slower career advancement and higher dropout rates. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle: the lack of women in leadership discourages the next generation from staying, which keeps leadership scarce. Research shows that mentorship programmes can improve promotion and retention rates for women in STEM by 15% to 38% - suggesting that the absence of mentorship has real consequences, and that targeted support does make a difference. Despite this, 35% of women with STEM degrees leave their fields within five years, compared to 26% of men - and workplace culture, not ability, is consistently cited as the main reason [11].

Bias in the Workplace

Once in STEM, women continue to face bias that directly affects their earnings. In the chemistry sector alone, around 20% of the wage gap between men and women has been linked to employer discrimination or gender bias - not to any difference in qualifications or performance [4]. Women are also often seen as less suited to high-pressure STEM environments such as IT, energy, or mining, which means fewer of them are promoted into leadership roles even when they are equally capable. This is often explained through the idea of "cultural fit" - a term that sounds reasonable but in practice tends to mean that the environment was built by and for men, and those in charge prefer to keep it that way.

What Is Being Done - and What Isn't

There has been real legislative progress. Austria has prohibited wage discrimination since 1979, and since 2014, larger companies have been required to publish income reports every two years [2]. In March 2023, the European Parliament passed binding pay transparency rules requiring companies to report gender pay gaps and take corrective action if the gap exceeds 5% [1]. These are meaningful steps.

But they are not enough on their own. Legislation can prohibit discrimination and require transparency, but it cannot by itself change the unconscious biases that affect hiring decisions, the social norms that push women toward part-time work, or the workplace cultures that quietly exclude women from the most valuable opportunities. The gap grew between 2010 and 2023 despite increasing awareness and policy efforts - which suggests that the deeper roots of the problem have not yet been seriously addressed.

Why This Should Matter to All of Us

For students at a university, many of whom are either already in STEM or heading toward fields that connect with it, this is not an abstract issue. The gap described here will be part of the professional environment we are about to enter. A 16% pay disparity in one of the most valued sectors of the economy is a sign that something is wrong - not just for the women who experience it directly, but for the fairness of the systems we are being trained to work within.

The gap did not persist because nobody noticed. It persisted because the changes required to close it go deeper than most institutions have been willing to go: into childhood education, workplace culture, the distribution of domestic labour, and the assumptions we make - often without realising it - about who belongs where. Raising awareness is a starting point. But awareness only becomes meaningful when it changes how we act, what we expect from the organisations we join, and what we are willing to demand.

What Can You Do to Fight the Gender Pay Gap

Even at university, we can start noticing and challenging inequality. It can be small things: who takes the lead in group projects, whose ideas are heard first, or who gets encouraged to pursue certain roles or internships. In STEM fields especially, stereotypes can still shape choices and confidence more than we realise - and this is part of why women's contributions are often valued less than they deserve, which feeds directly into the gender pay gap.

We do not need to fix everything as students, but we can stay aware, ask questions, and speak up when something feels unfair. We can also support each other, make space for different voices, and be open about opportunities and future careers.

Small actions taken now - in how we study, collaborate, and think about the future - can help build a more equal STEM environment for everyone.
